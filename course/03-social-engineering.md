# Social Engineering — When the Hacker Is a Smooth Talker

## What it is

**Social engineering** is hacking the *person* instead of the computer. No
malware, no password-guessing — just manipulation: lies, pressure, and
fake authority designed to make you hand over access, information, or
money yourself. **Pretexting** is the setup — inventing a believable story
("I'm from your bank's fraud department") to make the request sound
legitimate. The tech version you know best is the **tech-support scam**:
someone calls, texts, or pops up a scary browser warning claiming your
computer has a virus, then "fixes" it by getting remote access to your
machine.

## Why it matters

Security software can't stop you from being persuaded. Attackers know this,
so the human is the target: they research you from social media, spoof
caller ID so the number looks real, and script exactly what to say when you
hesitate. These scams work on smart, careful people — especially when
you're scared, rushed, or trying to be helpful. The defense is one habit:
**verify through a separate channel.** If someone contacts you claiming to
be your bank, your boss, or tech support — hang up, look up the real number
yourself, and call back. Every legit organization is fine with that. Scammers
are not.

## How the attack really works

**Example 1 — The tech-support cold call.** Your phone rings: "Hi, this is
David from Microsoft Support. We've detected a virus sending your personal
data overseas." He sounds professional and gives you a badge number. He
asks you to open your computer and go to a website that installs remote
access software (TeamViewer, AnyDesk, Quick Assist — all legitimate tools,
weaponized here). Once he's "looking at the problem," he shows you scary
(and completely normal) system logs as "proof" of infection, then asks for
payment for the fix — or quietly installs real malware, or has you log into
your bank "to verify the refund." Reality check: **Microsoft, Apple, and
Google will never cold-call you about a virus.** Ever.

**Example 2 — The grandparent scam.** "Grandma? It's me — I've been in a
car accident and I'm in jail. Please don't tell Mom and Dad. I need bail
money wired right now." The voice might even sound young and panicked (AI
voice cloning from a few seconds of social media video makes this worse).
The "public defender" or "officer" who gets on the line gives wiring
instructions and swears you to secrecy. The secrecy is the tell — it's
designed to stop you from verifying.

**Example 3 — The boss gift-card text.** You get a text from an unknown
number: "Hi, it's [your boss's name], I'm in a meeting and need you to grab
5 gift cards for client gifts. Scratch off the backs and send me photos of
the codes. I'll reimburse you." The attacker got your boss's name from the
company website or LinkedIn. Gift cards are untraceable cash — once you
send the codes, the money is gone in minutes.

**Example 4 — The fake utility worker / bank fraud call.** "This is the
fraud department at your bank — there's a $4,000 charge pending from
another state. To stop it, I need to verify your identity." They read off
your actual address (from a data breach) to sound real, then ask for your
card number, PIN, or the MFA code "we just texted you." That code is the
attacker logging into your real account *right now* — and you reading it to
them completes their login.

## Action checklist (do these today)

- [ ] **Memorize the golden rule:** hang up, look up the real number
      yourself (back of your card, official website, your contacts), call
      back. Never use a number, link, or callback the *caller* gave you.
- [ ] **Set a family code word.** Pick a word only your family knows. If
      "your grandchild" calls in trouble, ask for the code word. Takes two
      minutes, defeats the voice-clone scam.
- [ ] **Lock down what strangers can learn about you.** Set social media
      profiles to friends-only, and don't post travel plans, family member
      names, your boss's name, or "so proud of my new job at ___" publicly.
      Attackers build their scripts from exactly this.
- [ ] **Agree on a verification rule at work and at home:** any request
      involving money, gift cards, wire transfers, or password changes gets
      confirmed through a second channel (call the person, walk over, use a
      known number) — no exceptions, no matter how urgent it sounds.
- [ ] **Never grant remote access** to your computer or phone to someone
      who contacted *you*. If you called a company's real support line
      yourself, that's different.
- [ ] **Never read back an MFA code** to someone on the phone. Real support
      will never ask for it — anyone who does is mid-attack on your account.

## Red flags

- **Urgency + secrecy:** "Do this right now" combined with "don't tell
  anyone." Legitimate emergencies don't require secrecy from your family.
- **They contacted you first** about a problem you didn't know you had
  (virus, fraud, arrest).
- **Payment in gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, or cash apps** to
  strangers. No real company, agency, or relative-in-trouble needs iTunes
  cards.
- **Threats and fear:** "You'll be arrested," "your accounts will be
  frozen," "your computer will be wiped." Fear shuts down thinking — that's
  the point.
- **Authority name-dropping:** badge numbers, case numbers, "the IRS," "the
  FBI," "Microsoft Security." Real agencies don't collect payment over the
  phone, and real tech companies don't cold-call.
- **The story keeps changing** when you ask questions, or they get angry
  when you say "let me call you back at the official number."

## Quiz

**Q1.** Someone calls claiming to be from your bank's fraud department,
says there's a suspicious charge, and asks you to read back the
verification code they just texted you. What should you do?
A) Read it back — they already knew about the charge, so they're legit
B) Hang up, call the number on the back of your bank card, and ask about
   the charge
C) Ask for their badge number to confirm they're real
D) Give them the code but not your PIN

**Q2.** "Grandma, it's me, I'm in jail — please wire bail money and don't
tell Mom." What's the move that defeats this scam fastest?
A) Wire the money immediately — family comes first
B) Ask for the family code word, then hang up and call your grandchild's
   real number directly
C) Keep them on the phone while you drive to the bank
D) Ask what jail they're in and call that jail's main line from the number
   the caller gives you

**Q3.** Your "boss" texts from an unknown number asking you to buy gift
cards and send photos of the codes. Why is this always a scam?
A) Bosses never text
B) Gift card codes are untraceable, instant cash — no legitimate business
   operates this way, and the number isn't your boss's real one
C) Client gifts aren't a real thing
D) The text didn't use proper grammar

---

### Answer key

**Q1 — B.** The "verification code" is *your* bank's MFA code — reading it
to the caller completes the attacker's login. Real fraud departments never
ask for it. Always call back on a number you looked up yourself.

**Q2 — B.** The code word exposes an impostor instantly, and calling the
real number confirms your grandchild's actual situation. Never use contact
details the caller provides.

**Q3 — B.** The payment method is the giveaway: gift cards can't be
reversed or traced, which is exactly why scammers demand them. Verify any
money request through a separate, known channel.
