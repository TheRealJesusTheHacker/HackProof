# 02 — Smishing Basics: Don't Trust the Text

## Module Overview

**Level:** Foundations
**Time:** 20 minutes
**Prerequisites:** None

Smishing is phishing by text message. "Missed package," "bank alert," "toll due" — all designed to make you tap before you think. A text feels personal, and arrives where you talk to family; that trust is what the sender borrows. This module teaches the tells, the rules, and the one report that helps shut these campaigns down.

**LEARNING OBJECTIVES — After this module you can:**

- Name the four tells of a scam text
- Explain why a small fee is the trap
- Handle an unexpected login code correctly
- Report a scam text to 7726 (SPAM)
- Act correctly if you entered card or password details

## Lesson 1: The Tells

- **Links from strangers.** Especially shortened ones (bit.ly, t.ly), which hide where they go. Legit companies text from short codes, not random 10-digit numbers.
- **The fee trap.** "Pay $1.50 redelivery fee." The fee is not the scam — the card form is. A tiny charge feels too small to question, so you type your full card details into a page the sender controls. That card is then used for larger charges, or sold on.
- **The reply hook.** "Reply YES to confirm" or "Reply STOP to end messages." Either reply confirms your number is live and read by a human, making it worth more to the next sender. Do not reply — not even STOP.
- **Codes you didn't ask for.** A login code you did not request means someone is trying to log in as you, right now. The danger is anyone who then asks you to read it out — no company will. Enter a code only if you started the sign-in yourself.

### Case Breakdown: The Toll Notice

**What happened:** Priya gets a text: "Unpaid toll: $3.85. Pay within 12 hours to avoid a $50 penalty," with a link. The amount is small, and she drove a toll road last month. She pays. The $3.85 charge appears — followed days later by purchases she never made, on that card.

**Where it could have stopped:** At the link — toll agencies do not collect debts via texted links — or at the source: a random 10-digit number, not a short code.

**The signal most people miss:** The real $3.85 charge. It looks like proof the text was genuine. It is bait — a small charge keeps you from reporting the card while its details are used elsewhere.

### What to Do Instead — Checklist

- Stop. Do not tap or reply.
- Open the official app, or type the address yourself, and check there. No notice means the text was fake.
- Forward it to **7726** (SPAM), then block and delete.

> **COMMON MISTAKE:** Replying STOP. On a scam text, STOP is not an unsubscribe button — it signals that a person is listening.

## Lesson 2: The Rules

Senders change wording weekly. These rules do not change:

- Don't tap links in unexpected texts. Open the **official app** instead.
- Don't reply to scam texts, not even STOP. It confirms you're real. Block and report: forward to **7726** (SPAM).
- Your bank's real alerts never ask for passwords or full card numbers by text. It may ask if a charge was yours, yes or no. It will not send you to a page to "verify" details.
- Carriers, toll agencies, and the DMV don't collect debts via texted links. Your carrier never texts a payment link for your bill either — pay in the app or your account.
- If a text creates a deadline, treat the deadline as the tell. Real bills have due dates you can check in your account, not countdowns from a stranger.

### Case Breakdown: The Code Request

**What happened:** Ben gets a login code he did not request. A minute later a caller claiming to be from Ben's bank asks him to read out the code "to cancel a blocked sign-in." Ben reads it. The caller uses it, live, to finish signing in as Ben.

**Where it could have stopped:** At the first text. An unrequested code means an attack is in progress — sign in yourself, in the app, and change the password. Or at the request: a code is a key, and you never read a key to anyone.

**The signal most people miss:** The caller knew a code had just been sent, which felt like inside knowledge. He knew because he triggered it.

### Script: What to Say

If a caller asks for a code, password, or card number "to verify you," say: **"I don't give codes or passwords on calls or texts. I'll contact the company directly."** Then hang up. You need not be sure it is a scam before you refuse.

> **COMMON MISTAKE:** Treating a login code as harmless, like a reference number. A code is the second half of your password: whoever has both is you, as far as the account can tell.

## Hands-On Exercise

A text arrives: "USPS: your package could not be delivered. Pay the $1.50 redelivery fee here," followed by a shortened link.

**Decision point 1:** You are expecting a package. Does that clear it? No. Senders blast thousands of numbers; some recipients always are.
**Decision point 2:** The fee is only $1.50. Safe to pay? No. The amount is the bait; the card form is the trap.
**Decision point 3:** What do you do with the text? Forward it to 7726, block the number, delete it.

**Correct path:** Check tracking in the postal app or a site you type yourself — nothing there needs a fee. Report, block, delete.

## Knowledge Check

**1. What should you do first with a scam text?**
A. Reply STOP so they leave you alone
B. Tap the link to see if it looks real
C. Forward it to 7726, then block and delete it
D. Call the number back and ask who they are

**2. A text asks for a $1.50 redelivery fee. What is the scam?**
A. The $1.50 itself
B. The card form, which captures your full card details
C. A late fee added later
D. Nothing; small amounts are always safe

**3. A caller asks you to read out a login code you did not request, "to cancel a sign-in." What do you do?**
A. Read it — he is trying to help
B. Read only half of it
C. Hang up, sign in yourself in the app, and change your password
D. Ignore everything; a code alone means nothing is happening

### Answer Key

**1. C.** Forwarding to 7726 reports it to your carrier so the campaign can be blocked. A fails because any reply confirms your number is live.
**2. B.** The small fee makes the form feel safe. A fails because $1.50 is not the prize — your card details are.
**3. C.** The caller triggered the code and needs it to finish signing in as you. A fails: reading it out completes his login, it cancels nothing.

## Key Takeaways

- Urgency, a small fee, or a familiar name in a text is asking you to act before you check.
- Never tap the link. Open the official app or type the address yourself.
- Never reply — not even STOP. Forward to 7726 (SPAM), block, delete.
- Carriers, toll agencies, and the DMV do not collect money through texted links.
- A login code is a key. Never read one to a caller or send one in a text.
- If you entered card details, call your bank now; if a password, change it everywhere you reused it.

## Glossary

**Smishing** — Phishing by text message (SMS + phishing).
**Short code** — The short number legitimate companies text from.
**Shortened link** — A compressed link, such as bit.ly, that hides its destination.
**7726** — The number (spelling SPAM) for forwarding scam texts to your carrier for reporting.
**Login code** — A one-time code sent to prove a sign-in is yours.
**Look-alike domain** — An address made to resemble a real company's, with small changes.
