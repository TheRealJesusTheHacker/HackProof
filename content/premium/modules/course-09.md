MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Define smishing and explain why text messages bypass many email defenses
- Recognize bank, toll, delivery, and two-factor code lures on sight
- Apply a no-tap verification routine using official apps and typed addresses
- Report smishing by forwarding messages to 7726, and respond after engaging with one

LESSON 1 — WHY THE TEXT MESSAGE WORKS
Smishing is phishing by text message. It works because texts feel personal, arrive instantly, and are usually read within minutes. A short message also hides information: you cannot see a full sender address, links are disguised, and there is little context to make you suspicious. The major lures repeat a few themes. A bank alert says your account is locked. A toll notice says you owe a small amount and face penalties. A delivery message says a package is held and needs a fee or updated details. Each link leads to a fake site built to collect logins and card numbers. The small amounts are deliberate: a minor charge feels too small to be a scam, but the real target is the card number itself.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A driver receives a text saying an unpaid toll must be settled today to avoid a larger penalty, with a link to pay. The page looks like the toll authority and asks for his card. He pays a few dollars. This could have stopped at the link. His toll account, checked through the authority's official app, showed no balance due. The signal most people miss is the penalty deadline. Agencies bill account holders through their accounts and by mail; a same-day penalty threat by text link is the lure, not the process.

NO-TAP CHECKLIST: Never tap a link in an unexpected text about money, accounts, or deliveries. Open the official app yourself or type the address you know. When in doubt, call the organization on a number from its official site or your card.

COMMON MISTAKE: Replying to ask if the text is real. Replying confirms your number is active and attended. Do not engage with the sender at all.

LESSON 2 — THE CODE TRAP AND REPORTING THAT HELPS
The most dangerous smishing lure asks not for money but for a code. A scammer who already has your password triggers a real login code to your phone, then poses as the platform, a buyer, or support, asking you to share the code you just received. Sharing it hands over the second factor protecting your account. No legitimate company will ask for a code sent to you. Treat that code like a key: it belongs only on a login screen you reached yourself. Reporting is simple: forward the smishing text to 7726, which spells SPAM on a keypad. Your carrier uses these reports to block senders and patterns.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A marketplace seller gets a message from a buyer who says he sent a verification code to prove he is not a bot, and asks the seller to read it back. At that moment a real code arrives by text: it is for the seller's own email account. The seller nearly reads it out. This could have stopped at the request itself. A buyer never needs a code sent to your phone. The signal most people miss is timing. A code arriving when a stranger asks for one is not coincidence; it is the attack in progress.

WHAT TO DO AFTER ENGAGING: If you entered a password through a text link, change it from the official app and turn on multi-factor authentication. If you entered card details, contact your bank and replace the card. Then forward the original text to 7726 and report the attempt.

COMMON MISTAKE: Believing a message because it appears in an existing thread from your bank. Sender names and threads can be spoofed. Judge the request, not the thread it arrived in.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
At lunch you receive a text: your bank has locked your account after a suspicious charge; tap the link to confirm your identity within two hours. Decision point one: the message sits in a thread showing earlier, genuine bank alerts. The correct path is to distrust it anyway. Threads can be spoofed. Decision point two: instead of tapping, you open your bank app yourself. It shows no lock and no alert. The correct path is to accept that answer and stop. The app you opened is the authoritative source, not the text. Decision point three: a second text says a code was sent and asks you to enter it on the linked page. The correct path is to enter nothing, forward both texts to 7726, and delete them.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Bank, toll, and delivery lures harvest logins and card details, not small fees.
- Never share a verification code. It belongs only on a login you started.
- Verify in the official app you open, never through a link you were sent.
- Forward smishing texts to 7726, then delete them. Do not reply.

GLOSSARY
Smishing — Phishing delivered by text message
Verification Code — A short, temporary code proving you control an account or device
Spoofing — Faking a sender name, number, or thread so a message looks familiar
Credential Harvesting — Collecting usernames and passwords through a fake login page
7726 — The carrier reporting number for spam texts, spelling SPAM on a keypad
