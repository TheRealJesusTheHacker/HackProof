MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Take the first containment steps in the first hour after a scam
- Secure your email and bank accounts from a clean device
- Place a free credit freeze with all three bureaus
- Report the fraud and document everything

LESSON 1 — THE FIRST HOUR: ISOLATE AND CONTAIN
If you have just been scammed, you are not too late, and you are not alone. Speed matters more than perfection now. Your first job is to cut the scammer's access and stop further money movement. Write down the time you start, work in order, and keep notes as you go.

Isolate first. If you installed software the scammer asked for, gave remote access, or entered credentials through a link, disconnect that device and stop using it for banking or email. Do not delete anything yet. Messages, numbers, and receipts are evidence.

Next, from a clean device you trust, secure your email: change its password, sign out other sessions, and turn on multi-factor authentication. Email controls password resets for almost everything else, so it comes first. Then call your bank using the number on the back of your card, not a number from the scammer. Explain that fraud is in progress, ask what can be stopped or reversed, and ask for the case number. Money sent by wire, gift card, or payment app is harder to recover, which is why the call cannot wait.

Say this to your bank: "I was deceived into this transaction and I am reporting it as fraud now. What can be stopped, and what is my case number?"

CASE BREAKDOWN: A woman pays a fake support company and allows remote access. Ashamed, she waits two days before telling anyone, and saved card details in her browser are used twice in that time. What happened: the loss grew during the silence, not during the original call. Where it could have stopped: an immediate bank call and a disconnected computer would have limited the damage to the first payment. The signal most people miss: embarrassment is part of the scammer's design. They count on you not reporting quickly.

COMMON MISTAKE: Staying in contact with the scammer to recover the money. The fee they ask for to release a refund is a second scam.

LESSON 2 — FREEZE, REPORT, AND DOCUMENT
Once your money accounts are contained, protect your identity. Place a credit freeze with each of the three nationwide bureaus: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. A freeze restricts access to your credit file so a new loan or card cannot easily be opened in your name. Freezes are free, you can lift one temporarily when you apply for credit, and you must contact all three because each keeps its own file. A fraud alert is weaker: it asks lenders to take extra care but does not block access the way a freeze does.

Report what happened. File with the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov, or use identitytheft.gov if your identity was used; it builds a recovery plan and prefilled letters. File with the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov as well, and with local police if money was taken. Reporting rarely guarantees recovery, but it creates the paper trail banks and investigators ask for.

Document everything in one folder: screenshots, the numbers and names used, receipts, gift card numbers, bank case numbers, and a dated timeline in your own words. Save originals, not just summaries.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A man whose details were used to open a store card freezes credit with one bureau only. Months later a second card appears through a lender that checked a different bureau. Two files stayed open. A freeze at all three bureaus on the same day would have stopped it. The signal most people miss: there is no single switch. Three bureaus means three freezes.

COMMON MISTAKE: Relying on a fraud alert alone and assuming your credit is locked. Confirm each freeze and keep each bureau PIN safe.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Situation: This morning you paid a caller who claimed to be from your bank's fraud team, and read out a code from a text message before realizing the real bank never called.

Decision point 1: The caller phones again, offering a refund if you pay a processing fee. Correct path: hang up. Refund fees do not exist in legitimate recovery.
Decision point 2: Your laptop was used for the payment and the caller directed every step. Correct path: disconnect it, use a clean device to secure email first, then call your bank on the number from your card.
Decision point 3: The bank opens a case. Correct path: freeze credit at all three bureaus, file FTC and IC3 reports, and start your document folder while details are fresh.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Act in the first hour: isolate the device, secure email, then call your bank.
- Use contact numbers from your card, never from the scammer.
- Freeze credit free at all three bureaus, and keep each confirmation.
- Report to the FTC and IC3, and to police if money was taken.
- Document everything in one folder, while details are fresh.

GLOSSARY
Credit freeze — A free restriction that blocks access to your credit file at a bureau.
Fraud alert — A notice asking lenders to take extra care with your name.
Clean device — A trusted device not involved in the compromise.
Containment — Steps that stop a scammer's access and further loss.
IC3 — The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center.
