MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~15 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Explain what HTTPS protects on public Wi-Fi and what it does not
- Recognize an evil-twin network and refuse it
- Set your phone to prefer its own connection over unknown networks
- Handle banking, sign-ins, and downloads safely while traveling

LESSON 1 — WHAT PUBLIC WI-FI ACTUALLY EXPOSES
On an open café, hotel, or airport network, strangers share the same connection with no barrier between you. HTTPS, shown by the padlock in your browser, encrypts the content of your visit to a website, so a bystander cannot simply read your password or card number as it passes.

Know its limits. HTTPS does not prove the site itself is honest: a fake banking page can use it too. It does not hide which services you connect to, and it does nothing if you type details into a page reached through a fake network prompt. The padlock means the connection is encrypted, not that the site deserves your trust.

The safest habit while traveling is simple: prefer your phone's mobile data, or your own personal hotspot, for anything sensitive. Use public Wi-Fi for maps, news, and entertainment if you wish, and switch to your own connection for banking, email, and shopping. A reputable virtual private network can add protection on a network you must use, but it does not replace checking the site you are on.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A traveler joins an airport network and gets a sign-in page that looks official. He enters his email and password to get online. Nothing connects, so he tries another network and forgets it. What happened: the first page was a credential harvester. Where it could have stopped: a Wi-Fi sign-in page never needs your email account password. The signal most people miss: the request itself was wrong, however professional the page looked.

COMMON MISTAKE: Treating the padlock as a safety badge for the whole site. It only describes the connection, not the honesty of whoever runs the page.

LESSON 2 — EVIL TWINS AND AUTO-JOIN
An evil twin is a hotspot imitating a legitimate one, often with the same or a nearly identical name, such as Hotel Guest or Hotel Free Wi-Fi. Devices cannot tell the real one from the copy by name alone. Join the twin and your traffic passes through the attacker's equipment, where they can present fake login pages or offer a malicious software update.

Reduce the risk before you travel. Turn off auto-joining for open networks, and forget networks from past trips so your phone does not reconnect to a familiar name in a different place. At a hotel, confirm the exact network name and password with staff before you connect, and be suspicious if that name appears twice.

Never accept a prompt on public Wi-Fi asking you to install a profile, a certificate, or an app to get online, and do updates at home or on your own hotspot.

Defensive checklist:
- Ask staff for the exact network name, then check the list carefully.
- Turn off auto-join and forget old travel networks.
- Use mobile data or your own hotspot for banking, email, and shopping.
- Decline any install, profile, or update offered as the price of connecting.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A guest sees two networks with her hotel's name and picks the stronger signal. It works, so she checks her bank balance. What happened: signal strength is how evil twins win, because the attacker's hotspot is often closer than the real access point. Where it could have stopped: at reception, with one question about the exact name. The signal most people miss: a duplicate name is not a glitch. It is the warning.

COMMON MISTAKE: Leaving Wi-Fi on while walking around a city. Your phone can join networks on its own unless you change those settings.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Situation: You arrive at a hotel. Your phone offers three networks with similar names, and you need to check your bank balance before dinner.

Decision point 1: One network has the strongest signal and no password. Correct path: do not join yet. Ask the front desk for the exact network name and password.
Decision point 2: The desk confirms one name, and both copies still appear. Correct path: switch to your phone's mobile data for the bank check. The sensitive task does not need hotel Wi-Fi at all.
Decision point 3: A pop-up offers a security update you must install to keep browsing. Correct path: decline it, disconnect, and forget the network. Updates never arrive this way.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- HTTPS encrypts a connection. It does not vouch for the site at the other end.
- Prefer mobile data or your own hotspot for banking, email, and shopping.
- Confirm the exact network name with staff, and treat duplicates as a warning.
- Turn off auto-join and forget networks when you leave a place.
- Never install profiles, certificates, or updates offered over public Wi-Fi.

GLOSSARY
Public Wi-Fi — A shared wireless network offered in places such as cafés, hotels, and airports.
HTTPS — An encrypted connection between your browser and a website.
Evil twin — A fake hotspot imitating a legitimate network's name.
Personal hotspot — A private connection broadcast by your own phone for your devices.
Auto-join — A setting that connects your device to known or open networks without asking.
