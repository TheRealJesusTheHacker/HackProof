MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~25 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Explain why a unique password on every account limits the damage of any one breach
- Build a strong passphrase and store your passwords in a password manager
- Turn on multi-factor authentication and choose an authenticator app over text codes
- Explain SIM swapping, and secure your email and master password first

LESSON 1 — UNIQUE PASSWORDS AND THE MANAGER THAT REMEMBERS THEM
When a website is breached, its stolen usernames and passwords are tested automatically against other sites. One minor account sharing your email password hands an attacker both the password and the account that receives every reset link you own. A unique password on every account contains a breach to that account instead of opening your whole digital life. No one can memorize dozens of strong, unique passwords,. A password manager stores your logins in an encrypted vault, generates long random passwords, and fills them only on the correct site. A passphrase of several unrelated words, used on one account only, is the human-usable alternative. Length beats clever substitutions.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A streaming service a man barely uses is breached, exposing his email and password. Because he reused it, attackers log into his email. From the inbox they find his bank, reset its password using the email account itself, and begin moving money. This could have stopped at the reuse, years earlier. The signal most people miss is that the breached site did not matter. Low-value accounts are harvested precisely because their passwords unlock high-value ones.

SETUP CHECKLIST: Install a reputable password manager. Change your email password first, to a new unique passphrase. Then change financial logins, then the rest in order of importance, replacing every reused password with a generated one.

COMMON MISTAKE: Creating one excellent password and using it everywhere to be safe. Strength does not fix reuse. A single exposure, from any site however small, hands that password to attackers who will try it everywhere.

LESSON 2 — MULTI-FACTOR AUTHENTICATION, IN THE RIGHT ORDER
A password can be stolen without you knowing. Multi-factor authentication, or MFA, adds a second proof. But second factors are not equal. Texted codes are weakest because of SIM swapping: a criminal convinces a carrier to move your number to a SIM they control, so your calls and login codes go to them while your phone loses service. An authenticator app generates codes on your device itself, independent of your number, and is not defeated by a transfer. 

CASE BREAKDOWN: A woman notices her phone has shown no service all morning. By the time she reaches her carrier's store, her number has been transferred to a criminal who used texted codes to reset her email password and then her bank password. This could have stopped months earlier, when her bank offered app-based authentication and she kept text codes. The signal most people miss is the service loss itself. Unexplained loss of mobile service can be step one of an account takeover, and it deserves a same-day call to your carrier and your bank.

PRIORITY ORDER: Secure email first, with a unique passphrase and app-based MFA, because email resets everything else. Then secure financial accounts. Protect your password manager with your strongest passphrase and MFA; its master password is never reused anywhere.

COMMON MISTAKE: Turning on MFA and considering an account finished, while its recovery email is an old address protected by a reused password.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
You decide to secure your accounts this week and open your password manager. Decision point one: you must choose a master password. The correct path is a long, unique passphrase used nowhere else, written down and stored securely at home. Decision point two: the manager lists three accounts sharing one password: your email, your bank, and a shopping site. The correct path is to change email first, then the bank, then shopping, generating a different password for each. Email comes first because it controls resets for the rest. Decision point three: your bank offers MFA by text or by authenticator app. The correct path is the app.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Unique passwords contain breaches. Reuse turns one breach into every account.
- A password manager remembers strong passwords so you never need to reuse weak ones.
- Choose an authenticator app over texted codes. SIM swapping steals numbers, not devices.
- Secure email first, then financial accounts, then everything else in order.

GLOSSARY
Password Manager — A tool storing logins in an encrypted vault and generating unique passwords
Passphrase — A long password built from unrelated words, used on one account only
Multi-Factor Authentication — A login requiring a second proof beyond a password
Authenticator App — An app generating time-based login codes on your device
SIM Swap — Fraudulently transferring a victim's number to a SIM the criminal controls
Master Password — The single password unlocking a password manager, never reused elsewhere
