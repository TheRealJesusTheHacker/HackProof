MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Describe how romance scams build trust over weeks before any request for money
- Recognize the manufactured emergencies used to extract payment
- Explain why refusing video calls and meetings is a major warning sign
- Support a friend or family member in a possible romance scam without shaming them

LESSON 1 — TRUST FIRST, REQUEST LATER
A romance scam rarely starts with a request for money. It starts with attention. The scammer contacts you on a dating app, on social media, or through a supposedly wrong number, and then invests time. The person remembers details, mirrors your values, and talks about a future together. The profile is usually built from stolen photos and an invented biography. Each persona pre-explains why the person cannot meet or video call.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A woman meets a man on a dating app who says he is an engineer on a contract overseas. They message daily for six weeks. His camera is always broken and his schedule never allows a live video call. Then his equipment fails, his pay is frozen, and he will lose the contract unless a part is paid for this week. He asks her to send money, promising double repayment. This could have stopped at the weeks-long refusal to appear on live video. The signal most people miss is that every excuse pointed one direction: this person could receive money but could never be seen or verified.

BOUNDARY CHECKLIST: Insist on a live video call early, and treat repeated refusal as an answer, not a delay. Never send money, forward money for someone else, or share financial access for a person you have not met and verified in person.

COMMON MISTAKE: Believing long, emotional, detailed conversation proves a person is real. Time and warmth are the scammer's tools, not evidence of identity. 

LESSON 2 — THE EMERGENCY, THE PACKAGE, AND THE INVESTMENT PIVOT
Once trust is established, the request arrives as a crisis. A customs fee must be paid to release a package the scammer is sending you. A medical emergency requires money before treatment. A second path skips emergencies and pivots to opportunity: the partner reveals a talent for investing, often in cryptocurrency, and offers to help you earn too. 

CASE BREAKDOWN: A man in a three-month online relationship gets a call from a supposed customs officer. His partner, he is told, shipped him a box of cash and valuables, but a fee must be paid before release. He pays by wire. Days later a larger clearance charge appears, with a warning of legal trouble. This could have stopped at the first fee. Real customs processes do not run through a partner's shipment or demand urgent wire payments from the recipient. The signal most people miss is the threat after the request. When paying is framed as the only way to avoid legal danger, you are being managed, not informed.

WHAT TO SAY AND DO: Say: I care about you, and I do not send money to anyone I have not met and verified. That rule does not change in an emergency. If you have sent money, cut off contact, preserve messages and receipts, contact your bank, and report the fraud.

COMMON MISTAKE: Sending more small amounts to protect money already sent, hoping the story resolves. Every additional transfer is a new loss.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
You have talked for two months with someone who has declined three video calls. Tonight the person says a family member is in a hospital overseas and asks you to send money for urgent care, promising repayment next week. Decision point one: the request is emotional and time-sensitive. The correct path is to pause and apply your rule rather than evaluate the emergency, which you cannot independently confirm. Decision point two: the person sends a photo of a hospital corridor and an official-looking document. The correct path is to treat both as unproven. Neither replaces live verification. Decision point three: you decline, and the person says your refusal proves you never cared. The correct path is to end the conversation. A real partner respects a financial boundary; guilt used to reverse one is the scam defending itself.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Romance scams spend weeks building trust so the request feels like helping, not paying.
- A partner who can never video call, visit, or be verified is not verified.
- Never send, receive, or forward money for someone you have only met online.
- Support victims without shame. Secrecy and isolation are what the scam runs on.

GLOSSARY
Romance Scam — Fraud using a fake romantic relationship to extract money or favors
Persona — A fabricated identity built from stolen photos and an invented biography
Layering — A sequence of escalating fees or emergencies after an initial payment
Investment Pivot — Shifting a romantic connection into a fraudulent investment scheme
Money Mule — A person used to receive and forward stolen money, often unknowingly
