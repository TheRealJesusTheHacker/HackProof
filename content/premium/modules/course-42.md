Surveillance & Access Control Systems
MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: 60 minutes | Prerequisites: Structured Cabling & Physical Installs or equivalent.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Place cameras by purpose, field of view, and lighting
- Explain retention and remote viewing decisions plainly
- Compare credential types and door hardware
- Apply privacy and notice considerations to a design
- Design a small-business system on paper

LESSON 1 — CAMERAS: PLACEMENT, LIGHT, AND RETENTION
Start with a purpose for each camera: identify a person at a door, recognize activity in a lot, or observe a wide area. Purpose decides placement, height, and lens. Too high gives tops of heads; aimed at a bright doorway, silhouettes. Walk the site after dark, noting light and glare.

Field of view is a trade: wide coverage shows more area with less detail; narrow shows detail over less area. Overlap key views at entrances and cash areas.

Retention is how long recordings are kept before overwriting. It depends on storage, camera count, resolution, and motion. Ask how soon the client learns of incidents, size to that, and state the limit in writing. For remote viewing, use the manufacturer's platform, unique accounts, and multi-factor authentication where available; remove former employees promptly.

Camera design checklist:
- Write each camera's purpose before choosing hardware
- Check the scene in daylight and darkness
- Plan power, network, mounting, and weather rating
- Agree retention in writing; set unique remote accounts

CASE BREAKDOWN: A shop owner mounts eight cameras evenly. The register camera faces a window, so faces are dark. The back door camera is too high to identify anyone. Two watch the same empty wall. Fewer, purpose-aimed cameras would have cost less and identified more.

COMMON MISTAKE: Promising retention you have not calculated. Put the target and its limits in writing before install day.

LESSON 2 — ACCESS CONTROL, PRIVACY, AND NOTICE
Access control decides who may open which door, and when. The parts are a credential, a reader, a controller that checks permission, and electric door hardware. Credentials include cards, fobs, mobile credentials, PINs, and biometrics on some systems. Cards can be shared, PINs observed, phones die, and biometrics add privacy duties. Recommend by risk and turnover - high turnover favors credentials cheap to replace and fast to revoke.

Door hardware must match the door, its use, and life-safety rules. Exit-path doors must let people leave safely, including during fire alarm or power failure. Never design locking that traps occupants; involve the authority or licensed provider for fire and life-safety compliance when a design touches an exit.

Privacy is a design input. Cameras belong where the client has a legitimate purpose, not in restrooms or locker rooms. Audio recording carries separate legal restrictions in many places - do not enable it by default. Tell the client to post notice where required, limit who can view footage, and confirm current local requirements.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A manager asks for a hidden audio camera over a break room to investigate theft. Decline: break areas carry privacy expectations and audio adds legal risk. Offer visible cameras at the stockroom door and register, plus inventory controls.

COMMON MISTAKE: Giving everyone all-doors, all-hours access because it is easier. Default to least access, removed the day a person leaves.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Design on paper for a small business: storefront, back door, register, stockroom, and parking area, with six staff.
Decision point 1: The owner wants one wide camera for the whole interior. Correct path: Split the purpose - identification at entrance and register, wider observation for floor and stockroom.
Decision point 2: Staff ask for one shared back-door code. Correct path: Refuse it. Issue individual credentials, logged and revocable per person.
Decision point 3: The owner wants a former manager's remote viewing kept active. Correct path: Remove it. Remote viewing is for current, named users only.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Design every camera to a purpose; check it day and night
- State retention honestly and in writing before install
- Individual credentials only - logged, scheduled, revocable
- Never design locking that traps occupants on an exit path
- Privacy and notice are design inputs

GLOSSARY
Field of view - Area a camera can see from its position and lens
Retention - How long recordings are kept before overwriting
Credential - What a person presents to request entry, such as a card or PIN
Controller - Hardware that checks a credential and releases the door
Least access - Only the doors and hours a job requires
Backlight - Bright light behind a subject, leaving the face in shadow
