MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Explain how voice clones, face-swap video, and AI-written messages are used in scams
- Apply a family code word and callback verification before acting on an urgent request
- Describe why spotting glitches is not a reliable defense
- Use a short script to pause a request politely

LESSON 1 — WHEN THE VOICE IS REAL AND THE PERSON IS NOT
Voice cloning needs only a short sample of someone speaking. A public video can give a tool enough to imitate a voice, including its pace and emotion. Face-swap video does the same with a face. AI writing removes the old warning signs too: flawed grammar can now read cleanly, in the right tone for a boss, a bank, or a relative.

Do not make spotting a glitch your main plan. A clone may sound flat one day and flawless the next, and your ear cannot be trained to win that contest reliably. Treat convincing audio and video as unproven until you verify the person through a separate channel you control.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A parent gets an evening call. The voice sounds exactly like her adult son, who says he has been in a car accident. A man claiming to be a lawyer asks for payment right away, and she is told not to call anyone else. What happened: a cloned voice wrapped in urgency and secrecy. Where it could have stopped: at the demand for immediate payment. The signal most people miss: a real emergency survives a two-minute verification. A scam often does not.

Agree a family code word in advance, one that would not come up in normal conversation, and never put it in a message thread or online. If a caller claiming to be family cannot give it, end the call and phone the real person on a number you already have saved.

Say this: "I want to help. Our rule is that I call you back on your saved number and we use our code word."

COMMON MISTAKE: Asking questions only the real person would know, but using facts that are public online, such as a pet name or a birthday. A code word works because it exists only inside your family.

LESSON 2 — AI-WRITTEN MESSAGES AND FAKE VIDEO CALLS
AI-written phishing looks ordinary, and that is the point. An email can now copy the style of a delivery notice or an executive request with no obvious errors. On a video call, a scammer can present a swapped face, keep the meeting brief, blame a poor connection, and move quickly to a payment request.

Move the interaction to ground you control. For a work request, start a new message to the person's known address instead of replying to the one in front of you. For a video request involving money or secrecy, end the call and restart contact through a saved number.

Defensive checklist:
- Pause whenever money, gift cards, or login codes are requested.
- End the suspicious contact. Do not argue inside it.
- Reach the real person through a number or address you already trust.
- Refuse secrecy. A request that cannot be checked with anyone else is a warning sign by itself.

CASE BREAKDOWN: An employee joins a video call that appears to include a senior leader, whose face and voice look right. The leader calls a deal confidential and asks for gift cards, with the codes sent before a deadline. What happened: a face-swap decorated an old gift card scam. Where it could have stopped: at the secrecy and the payment method. No legitimate deal is settled with gift card codes. The signal most people miss: the technology was new, but the ask was not.

COMMON MISTAKE: Staying on the call to gather proof. You do not need proof. Ending contact and verifying separately is enough.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Situation: Your phone shows your sister's name. A voice that sounds like hers says she is stranded and needs money sent to a driver who is waiting. She begs you not to tell your parents.

Decision point 1: She needs the money in ten minutes. Correct path: do not send it. Urgency is a pressure tool, not evidence. Say you will call back on her saved number.
Decision point 2: She cannot give the code word and says you are wasting time. Correct path: end the call. Identity is not verified, whatever the voice sounds like.
Decision point 3: You call her saved number and she answers at home, fine. Correct path: tell her what happened, note the number that called, and report the attempt. Verification outside the frightening call protected you.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- A familiar voice or face is no longer proof of identity on its own.
- Verify through a separate channel, using a number you already trust.
- Use a private family code word for emergency requests.
- Secrecy, urgency, and unusual payment methods are warning signs. Judge the request, not the realism.

GLOSSARY
Voice clone — Generated speech that imitates a person's voice.
Face-swap video — Video in which one person's face is digitally replaced with another's.
Deepfake — Synthetic audio or video made to look like a real recording.
Code word — A private word agreed in advance to confirm a family member's identity.
Callback verification — Ending a contact and calling back on a saved number.
