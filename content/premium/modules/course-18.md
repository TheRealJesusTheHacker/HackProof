MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Explain how a SIM swap moves your number to a scammer's phone
- Set a carrier PIN and port-out protection on your account
- Reduce the accounts a stolen number can unlock
- Recover in the right order if your number is swapped

LESSON 1 — HOW A SIM SWAP WORKS
Many accounts treat your phone number as proof of identity, sending password resets and one-time codes by text. A SIM swap breaks that assumption. A scammer convinces your carrier, or uses stolen personal details, to move your number to a SIM they control. Your phone loses service. Their phone starts receiving your calls and texts, including the codes meant to keep strangers out.

The warning sign is sudden and specific: your phone shows no service, or emergency calls only, where you normally have coverage, and restarting does not fix it. Treat unexplained loss of service as a possible security event, not just a network fault.

Know about number barnacles too. Old numbers and forgotten recovery options stay attached to accounts for years, quietly offering extra ways in. An outdated recovery phone is a door you forgot you left unlocked. Review recovery details on your email, bank, and carrier accounts and remove anything no longer yours.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A man notices his phone lose signal during dinner. He assumes an outage and waits. By morning, texts he never saw have reset his email and bank passwords. The quiet hours were the working hours of a SIM swap. Where it could have stopped: at the loss of service. A call to the carrier that evening, from another phone, could have frozen the change early. The signal most people miss: no service on your phone alone, while everyone around you has service, is information about your phone specifically.

COMMON MISTAKE: Waiting until morning to see if service returns. Delay is the resource the scammer spends. Act the same hour.

LESSON 2 — LOCK THE NUMBER, THEN REBUILD TRUST
Start at the carrier. Set a PIN on your mobile account that must be given before changes are made, and use one that appears nowhere else. Ask specifically about port-out protection, or a number lock, which blocks your number being moved without extra verification, and turn it on. A strong PIN only helps if you do not share it: your carrier will never ask you to read it out on an unsolicited call.

Then reduce what a number can unlock. Wherever an account offers an authenticator app or a security key, prefer that over text messages. App-based codes live on your physical device, so moving your number does not move your codes. Work through accounts in order: email first, then banking, then anything that can spend or borrow. Text codes beat no second factor, but treat them as the weakest acceptable option.

If you are swapped despite this, order matters. First, contact your carrier from another phone or in store and regain control of the number. Second, from a clean device, secure your email with a new password and app-based authentication. Third, secure bank accounts and tell your bank to watch for fraud. Fourth, work through remaining accounts, changing passwords and reviewing recovery details.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A woman has a carrier PIN and port-out protection set, and her bank uses an authenticator app. A swap attempt fails the PIN check, and she is notified about the attempt. Layered defenses failed closed instead of open, at the carrier, before any account was reachable. The signal most people miss: good protection is mostly invisible. Set it before you need it.

COMMON MISTAKE: Using the same PIN for your carrier account as for your debit card or phone unlock. One leaked number then unlocks all of them.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Situation: In the evening your phone drops to emergency calls only. Your partner's phone, on the same carrier, has full service. You are tempted to wait until morning.

Decision point 1: You restart the phone and service does not return. Correct path: treat this as a possible SIM swap now. Your partner's working phone is the evidence.
Decision point 2: You call your carrier from your partner's phone. Correct path: report a suspected unauthorized SIM change, ask for the number to be secured, and note the reference given.
Decision point 3: The carrier confirms a swap and restores your number. Correct path: do not stop there. Secure email, then bank accounts, and move important accounts to app-based authentication.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- A SIM swap moves your number and its reset codes to a scammer.
- Set a unique carrier PIN and turn on port-out protection before you need it.
- Treat unexplained loss of service as a security event, and act the same hour.
- Prefer authenticator apps or security keys over text codes.
- Remove old numbers and recovery options from important accounts.

GLOSSARY
SIM swap — Moving a number to another SIM without the owner's permission.
Port-out protection — A carrier setting that blocks transferring a number unverified.
Carrier PIN — A passcode required before changes to a mobile account.
Number barnacle — An outdated recovery option still attached to an account.
Authenticator app — An app that generates sign-in codes on your device.
