Leadership, Projects & Service Management
MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: 60 minutes | Prerequisites: Technical team experience or Sentinel baseline modules.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Define a project: scope, stakeholders, schedule, risk
- Distinguish incidents from problems; apply change control
- Explain an SLA commitment
- Lead a small technical team
- Build a documentation culture

LESSON 1 — PROJECT FUNDAMENTALS
A project is temporary work with a defined result, succeeding on four fundamentals. Scope is what is included - and, written down, what is not. Stakeholders: client sponsor, users, your team, anyone who can stop the work. The schedule is the honest task sequence with owners, dates, and testing time. Risk is what might go wrong, named early.

When a client asks for work outside written scope, do not absorb it silently or refuse it flatly. Assess cost, time, and risk, and agree the change in writing before doing it. Most project arguments are about a surprise, not the work.

Confirm the plan at kickoff, update stakeholders regularly, flag delays the day you see them, and close against the original scope.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A camera install is quoted for one building. On day two the owner asks the crew to "also do the warehouse." The lead agrees verbally, the job doubles, the deadline is missed, and the owner disputes the unwritten bill. The fix: write the added scope, price and schedule it as a change, get approval, then proceed.

Kickoff checklist:
- Written scope with explicit exclusions
- Named client sponsor and decision-maker
- Tasks with owners and dates; top risks with response plans
- Communication rhythm and change process agreed

COMMON MISTAKE: Starting on a handshake scope to seem easy to work with. Written scope is what easy to work with actually looks like.

LESSON 2 — SERVICE MANAGEMENT AND LEADING THE TEAM
An incident is an unplanned interruption - a camera offline, a user locked out - restored quickly and recorded. A problem is the underlying cause behind incidents. Fix the incident today; if it repeats, open the problem and remove the root cause. Teams that only fix incidents stay busy while nothing improves.

Change control alters a live service without breaking it: propose the change, consider risk and rollback, approve at the level its risk deserves, schedule it, and record it. Emergency changes are still recorded and reviewed after. Rule: no silent changes to client systems.

An SLA (service level agreement) is a written commitment on response and resolution targets and their measurement. Staff to it, track against it, and tell the client early when you risk missing it. Leading a small team is clarity: assign owners, check blockers, sequence work honestly, and give credit and correction specifically.

Write tickets and as-builts as if the next technician is a stranger at 2 a.m. - it may be you. Document decisions and reasons, not just actions.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A door controller fails three Fridays in a row, each time restored within the hour and closed as a success. On the fourth failure, the lead opens a problem record: a failing power supply, replaced once, ending the pattern. Good incident responses hid one unfixed problem.

COMMON MISTAKE: Closing repeat incidents as routine. A repeat is a problem announcing itself - escalate the pattern.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Plan on paper: you lead a three-person team on a two-week access control install, then support it under an SLA.
Decision point 1: The client will not name a decision-maker. Correct path: Do not start without one. Get a named sponsor in writing.
Decision point 2: The client requests six extra doors verbally. Correct path: Change control - written scope, price and schedule impact, written approval, then build.
Decision point 3: The same reader fails twice in two weeks, restored quickly each time. Correct path: Meet the SLA, and open a problem record to remove the cause.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Written scope, named stakeholders, honest schedule, named risks
- Changes are welcome - in writing, priced, approved first
- Fix incidents fast; open problems to remove causes
- An SLA is a promise with math behind it - track to it
- Document for the stranger at 2 a.m.; it may be you

GLOSSARY
Scope - Work a project includes, and what it does not
Stakeholder - Anyone affected by, or able to affect, a project
Incident - Unplanned interruption, restored as quickly as possible
Problem - Underlying cause behind one or more incidents
Change control - Proposing, approving, and recording changes
SLA - Service level agreement; written service targets
