MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~18 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Spot a stolen rental listing and a fake landlord
- Verify who actually owns a rental property
- Refuse deposits and application traps safely
- Protect your Social Security number during a search
- Report a rental scam with useful evidence

LESSON 1 — STOLEN LISTINGS AND THE LANDLORD WHO IS NEVER THERE
Rental scams start with a real home. A scammer copies photos from a legitimate listing or a home for sale, lowers the price, and reposts it. The deal makes you hurry: below-market rent, utilities included, available now if you act today.

The landlord is always elsewhere: out of state, overseas, or in the military. That story explains why no one can show the home. You get a drive-by instruction or a promise that keys will be mailed after payment. Pressure replaces access, and questions are met with a warning that another renter is ready to pay.

CASE BREAKDOWN: Marcus finds a house listed $400 below similar homes. The landlord says he relocated and will hold it if Marcus sends a $900 deposit by Zelle today. Marcus drives by. The house is real, and a lockbox is on the door, which feels reassuring. He pays. The listing was copied, the lockbox belongs to the real agent, and the scammer has no right to rent the home. Where it could have stopped: before payment, at the refusal of a viewing with someone who could open the door and prove authority. The signal most people miss: a real exterior proves nothing. Photos, addresses, and lockboxes can all be borrowed.

Payments by Zelle, wire, or gift card are effectively irreversible. Your rule: never pay before an in-person viewing inside the home, with a person who proves they own or manage it. Urgency is not a reason to skip that step. It is the reason to take it.

COMMON MISTAKE: Paying a small holding fee because it feels low-risk. That fee is often the whole goal, and first month rent may be requested next.

LESSON 2 — VERIFY OWNERSHIP AND PROTECT YOUR IDENTITY
Verify ownership yourself. Search county assessor or recorder records for the address, public in most counties, and compare the owner name with the person asking for money. Search the exact address online: the same home often appears at a higher price from a real brokerage. Call that brokerage on a number you find yourself.

Check proof calmly: a management company you can look up independently, a lease naming the verified owner, and a viewing inside. Walk away from anyone who refuses or rushes you.

The second theft is identity theft. Fake applications ask for your Social Security number, bank details, pay stubs, and license copies before you have seen the home. That packet can open accounts in your name. Submit a full application only after viewing the home and verifying the manager, through the screening company's known site, not a link from a stranger.

If you already paid or applied, contact your bank, save the listing, messages, and receipts, and report to the FTC and FBI IC3. If you shared your Social Security number, place free credit freezes at all three bureaus. A legitimate landlord can survive verification. A scammer cannot.

COMMON MISTAKE: Trusting a scanned lease, deed, or license sent by the landlord as proof. Documents are easy to copy or forge. Verification only counts when it comes from county records or another source you reached independently.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
You find an apartment well below market. The landlord says she is out of state, offers to mail keys after the deposit, and sends an application asking for your Social Security number.
Decision point 1: She will hold it for two hours if you send $500 by Zelle. Correct path: Refuse. No viewing inside and no verified ownership means no deposit, however short the deadline.
Decision point 2: You find the same photos listed by a brokerage at a higher rent. Correct path: Call the brokerage on a number you find yourself and trust the conflict as a warning, not the cheaper listing.
Decision point 3: You already sent the application. Correct path: Save all messages, report the scam, and place free credit freezes at all three bureaus to limit identity theft.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Below-market rent plus an absent landlord is a classic warning pair
- Never pay before viewing the home inside and verifying the owner
- Check county records and search the address for the real listing
- Do not send a full application to an unverified landlord
- Zelle and wire deposits to scammers are effectively irreversible

GLOSSARY
Stolen listing — A copied rental ad using photos and details from a real property
County assessor — The public office that keeps property ownership and tax records
Holding fee — Money demanded to reserve a home, often the scammer's main target
Credit freeze — A free block that stops new credit accounts being opened in your name
Lockbox — A key holder on a door that scammers may point to as false proof of access
Screening — The background and credit check a legitimate manager runs on applicants
