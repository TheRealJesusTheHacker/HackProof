MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Spot fake invoices and requests with changed payment details
- Explain payroll diversion and vendor email compromise
- Apply a dual-approval rule and callback checks to business payments
- Separate duties so no single message can move company money

LESSON 1 — FAKE INVOICES AND CHANGED DETAILS
Small businesses run on trust and speed, and payment scams use both. A fake invoice copies a real supplier's branding, uses a plausible amount, and arrives when payment is expected. The fraud often sits in one quiet difference: the bank details or payment link have changed. Everything else looks routine, because routine is the camouflage.

Treat payment details as sensitive data, not paperwork. Keep a verified record of each vendor's payment information, set up independently when the relationship starts. When an invoice says details have changed, stop. Do not use the phone number on that invoice. Call the vendor on the number in your verified record and confirm the change out loud. Legitimate vendors understand this call. Only a scammer pressures you to skip it.

Also match every invoice to something real before paying: an order, a delivery, or a service you can confirm happened.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A bookkeeper receives an invoice from a regular supplier, with a note that the supplier has changed banks. She updates the details and pays. The real supplier later asks about the unpaid invoice. What happened: the payment followed a compromised or imitated email, and the changed bank was the whole scam. Where it could have stopped: one callback to the verified number before updating anything. The signal most people miss: the change-of-bank note itself, presented as administration, is the moment of the crime.

COMMON MISTAKE: Replying to the suspicious email to ask if it is genuine. A compromised inbox will happily confirm itself. Start a fresh contact through a trusted channel.

LESSON 2 — PAYROLL DIVERSION AND VENDOR EMAIL COMPROMISE
Payroll diversion targets employees' pay. A message appearing to come from an employee asks payroll to update direct deposit details before the next run. The new account belongs to the scammer, and the employee discovers it on payday, when the wages are gone. The defense mirrors the vendor rule: no bank changes by email alone. Confirm with the employee in person or by a call to a number already on file.

Vendor email compromise goes further. The attacker gets into a real vendor's mailbox and learns your billing rhythm, invoice formats, and who approves what. Then, at the natural moment in a real transaction, a message from the genuine thread asks for payment to a new account. Because the context is real, your guard is down. Verification must be procedural, not based on how genuine a message feels.

Adopt a dual-approval rule: any payment above a limit you set, and any change to payment details at any amount, needs two people. One prepares it, a second checks the verified details and approves it. Write the rule down, and make following it a sign of diligence, never of distrust.

CASE BREAKDOWN: Before payday, payroll gets a polite email from an employee's correct address asking to switch banks. Payroll updates it. What happened: the employee's mailbox was compromised, so the address was real and the instruction was not. Where it could have stopped: a quick call to the employee's number on file. The signal most people miss: correct address and correct timing can both be supplied by a compromised account.

COMMON MISTAKE: Applying careful checks only to large payments. One detail change can redirect every payment that follows.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Situation: On a busy Friday, a regular vendor emails that they have changed banks and attaches a correct-looking invoice due today.

Decision point 1: The amount matches a delivery you received. Correct path: that confirms the invoice, not the bank details. The change still needs verification.
Decision point 2: You find the vendor's number on the emailed invoice. Correct path: do not use it. Call the number in your verified record instead.
Decision point 3: The vendor knows nothing about a bank change. Correct path: do not pay, preserve the email, warn staff, and tell the vendor through your normal channel so they can investigate a possible compromise.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Changed payment details are the high-risk moment.
- Verify changes by calling a number from your own records.
- Match invoices to real orders or deliveries before paying.
- Payroll bank changes are never confirmed by email alone.
- Use dual approval for payments and detail changes.

GLOSSARY
Fake invoice — A billing document made or altered by a scammer to divert payment.
Vendor email compromise — Fraud using a vendor's mailbox to redirect payments.
Payroll diversion — Redirecting an employee's wages by changing direct deposit details.
Dual approval — A rule requiring two people to approve a payment or change.
Verified record — Payment and contact details you confirmed independently.
