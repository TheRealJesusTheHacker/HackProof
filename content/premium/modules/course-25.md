MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Advanced | Time: ~25 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Secure email first from a clean device in hour one
- Kill active sessions and stop account access in the right order
- Protect bank, carrier, and credit accounts the same day
- Find hidden inbox rules and forwarding
- Document and report a takeover safely

LESSON 1 — HOUR 0 TO 1: EMAIL FIRST, FROM A CLEAN DEVICE
An account takeover means someone else controls an account in your name. Email is the master key: password resets for bank, shopping, and social accounts all land there. If the attacker keeps your email, they can undo every fix. So email comes first.

Work from a clean device you trust, not the one used to click the suspicious link. In hour zero to one, change your email password to a new, unique one, turn on two-factor authentication, and sign out all other sessions and devices from the security page. Remove connected apps you do not recognize.

CASE BREAKDOWN: Jordan gets bank alerts for transfers he did not make and finds sent emails he never wrote. His instinct is to change his bank password on his laptop, the device where he opened a malicious attachment. Instead, he uses his phone, which was not involved. He secures email first: new password, two-factor on, all sessions signed out. Only then does he call his bank. Where this could have failed: resetting passwords on the compromised device, in the wrong order, while the attacker still had a live email session to reset them back. The signal most people miss: a password change alone is not enough. Old sessions can stay signed in unless you end them.

Do not delete suspicious emails or alerts. They are evidence and a timeline. Note what you saw, switch devices, and start the clock.

COMMON MISTAKE: Starting with social media accounts. Email controls their recovery, and an attacker holding it can reclaim them in minutes.

LESSON 2 — HOURS 1 TO 4 AND THE SAME DAY: MONEY, CARRIER, CREDIT
Hours one to four, protect the money. Call your bank and card issuers on the numbers on your cards. Report the takeover, dispute transfers you did not make, and ask about holds and new credentials. Change passwords for other financial accounts from the clean device, in order of damage they could do, with two-factor on.

Call your mobile carrier the same day. Attackers intercept reset codes and port numbers to a SIM they control. Ask the carrier to add security and confirm your SIM has not changed.

Still the same day, place a free credit freeze at each of the three bureaus. Then reset remaining passwords, each unique. Check email settings for inbox rules that forward, delete, or hide messages, and for forwarding addresses you did not add. Attackers set these so alerts vanish while they work. Remove them, then change the email password once more if you found any.

Document as you go: times, contacts, case numbers, amounts, and screenshots on the clean device. Get a recovery plan at IdentityTheft.gov, report at reportfraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI IC3.

What not to do: Do not pay anyone offering to recover the account or money. Do not reuse the old password. Do not wait until tomorrow because alerts stopped. Silence can mean the attacker changed where alerts go.

COMMON MISTAKE: Freezing credit at one bureau only. A freeze must be placed at each bureau separately; one does not cover the others.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
At noon you discover reset emails you did not request, and a forwarding address you do not recognize.
Decision point 1: Your laptop, where you clicked a bad link, is open. Do you start resets there. Correct path: No. Switch to a clean device, secure email first, and sign out all sessions.
Decision point 2: Email is secured. Do you reset shopping accounts next. Correct path: No. Call your bank and carrier next, then place same-day freezes at all three bureaus.
Decision point 3: You find a rule deleting bank alerts. Correct path: Save a screenshot, remove the rule and forwarding, and change the email password again.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Secure email first, from a clean device, and sign out all sessions
- Hours one to four: call banks and card issuers, then your carrier
- Same day: free credit freezes at all three bureaus, in that order of urgency
- Check inbox rules and forwarding; attackers hide alerts there
- Document everything, report to the FTC and IC3, and pay no recovery offers

GLOSSARY
Account takeover — When an attacker controls an account in your name
Clean device — A trusted device not involved in the compromise
Session — A signed-in connection that can persist after a password change
Two-factor authentication — A second proof, beyond a password, required to sign in
Credit freeze — A free block at a credit bureau on new accounts in your name
Inbox rule — An automatic email action, such as forwarding or deleting messages
SIM change — Moving a phone number to a new SIM, which attackers use to intercept codes
Recovery plan — The report generated at IdentityTheft.gov
