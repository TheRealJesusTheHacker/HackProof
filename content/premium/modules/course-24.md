MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: ~22 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Recognize spoofed and lookalike emails
- Explain invoice bank changes and payroll diversion
- Verify any payment change out of band on a known number
- Apply dual approval for payment changes without IT staff
- Build a one-page payment policy your team follows

LESSON 1 — ONE CONVINCING EMAIL CAN MOVE THE MONEY
Business email compromise, or BEC, is fraud by email that looks like normal business. A criminal spoofs your executive, copies a real vendor thread, or registers a lookalike domain differing by one letter. The message is short, polite, and urgent: Please update our bank details, or, Change my direct deposit account before Friday payroll.

Small businesses are targeted because one person may receive invoices, approve payments, and run payroll, with no IT staff to catch a fake. The email arrives at a believable moment: during a real project, or while the owner travels.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A bookkeeper at a nine-person firm gets an email appearing to come from a regular supplier, in a thread like their prior invoices. It gives new bank details for a $23,400 invoice due that week. She updates the record and pays. The domain had one extra letter. The real supplier calls two weeks later asking why its invoice is unpaid. Where it could have stopped: at the bank-detail change, verified by phone on a number the firm already had, never a number in the email. The signal most people miss: the request changed the destination, not just the amount. Destination changes are where BEC steals.

Payroll diversion works the same way: a fake employee email updates direct deposit, and the next paycheck goes to the criminal. Wire payments are effectively irreversible once accepted, so if money was sent, call the bank immediately. Minutes matter more than a perfect investigation.

COMMON MISTAKE: Replying to the email to ask if it is real. That asks the criminal to confirm his own lie, in the channel he controls.

LESSON 2 — OUT-OF-BAND VERIFICATION AND DUAL APPROVAL
You do not need an IT department. You need two rules no urgency can override.

Rule one: out-of-band verification. Any request to change bank or payroll details is verified on a separate channel using contact details you already trust. Call the vendor, executive, or employee on a previously known number from your records, not from the email signature. Say: We received a request to change payment details. Did you send it. An email reply or text to a new number does not count.

Rule two: dual approval. One person enters a change and a second approves it before money moves. That second person can be the owner, but never the same person who received the email. Apply both rules to executive gift card requests, wires, and new vendor setup.

Write it on one page and post it where payments are made: no change by email alone, the callback script, known vendor numbers, the two approvers, and the response if money was sent: call the bank at once, then report to the FBI IC3 and FTC.

Teach staff that slowing down a payment change is doing the job correctly, even when the sender demands secrecy or speed. A real executive will accept the callback. A criminal will argue with the policy itself. Review vendor and payroll changes monthly for duplicates and changed accounts.

COMMON MISTAKE: Making an exception for the owner. BEC depends on such exceptions. The owner still gets a callback on the known number.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
You run payroll for a 12-person shop. Thursday at 4 p.m., an email from an address one letter off from your manager's asks you to update an employee's direct deposit before Friday, confidentially.
Decision point 1: The name and tone look right. Do you make the change. Correct path: No. Payroll changes require a call on a known number, plus a second approver.
Decision point 2: A reply says yes, please hurry. Correct path: Ignore it as verification. It came through the same untrusted channel.
Decision point 3: A payment already went to a changed account. Correct path: Call the bank immediately, preserve the emails with headers, and report to the FBI IC3.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- BEC steals by changing where money goes, not by hacking the bank
- Lookalike domains and spoofed threads make one email feel routine
- Verify every bank or payroll change by phone on a known number
- Require dual approval so no one email can move money alone
- If money was sent, call the bank immediately, then report

GLOSSARY
Business email compromise — Fraud using spoofed or lookalike business email to redirect payments
Spoofing — Faking a sender address to appear as someone else
Lookalike domain — An address differing from a real one by a letter
Out-of-band verification — Checking a request through a separate, already trusted channel
Dual approval — Requiring two people to enter and approve a change
Payroll diversion — Redirecting an employee's direct deposit to a criminal's account
Invoice fraud — A fake invoice that sends payment to the wrong account
