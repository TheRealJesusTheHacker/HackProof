MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~18 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Recognize fake charge, refund, and purchase confirmation notices
- Explain the overpayment trick and why the original payment fails
- Refuse screen-sharing and send-back requests during a refund
- Verify a charge through your own account instead of a message link
- Limit damage if you already shared access

LESSON 1 — THE CHARGE THAT NEVER HAPPENED
This scam starts with alarm. An email, text, or call says you were charged hundreds of dollars for a renewal or purchase you do not recognize, from a trusted name like Amazon or PayPal. A confirmation number and urgent phone number make it feel real. The notice is fake. Its job is to make you call the scammer posing as support, who offers a refund and walks you to your computer. A billing scare becomes access to your device and bank account.

CASE BREAKDOWN: Priya gets an email confirming a $749 order she never made, with a number to call. The agent says he can refund it if she opens her bank site while he is connected by remote access. She sees a refund arrive, but for $7,490. The agent panics, says he will lose his job, and begs her to send back the difference by Zelle and gift cards. In fact, he used her bank session to move $7,490 between her own accounts. No company money arrived. Where it could have stopped: at calling a number in an unsolicited email, and again at the remote access request. The signal most people miss: a real refund never requires you to log in while support watches, or to fix a company error with gift cards.

A payment that appears in your account can also reverse later. Funds shown as available does not mean the payment was genuine and collected.

COMMON MISTAKE: Treating a confirmation number as proof of a charge. It was typed by the sender. Your proof is your own account, opened by you.

LESSON 2 — THE SEND-BACK TRAP AND SAFE VERIFICATION
The overpayment works on kindness and fear. The scammer creates a too-large refund and pressures you to return the difference by Zelle, wire, gift cards, or crypto. Those methods are effectively irreversible. When the fake payment reverses, or you realize the money came from your own savings, the sent-back money is gone.

Screen-sharing makes it worse. With remote access, a scammer can alter what a bank page appears to show, open accounts, and steal passwords while you watch a supposed refund. Never install remote access or share your screen at the direction of someone who contacted you about a charge.

Use this sequence instead. Do not call the number in the message. Open your bank, Amazon, or PayPal account yourself, by typing the address or using your saved app. If the charge is not there, there is nothing to refund. If unsure, call the number on your card and ask: Is this charge on my account.

Say this: I do not process refunds on inbound calls. I will contact my bank directly. Then hang up. If you already gave access, disconnect from the internet, contact your bank from a clean phone, change key passwords from a clean device, and save the messages for the FTC and FBI IC3.

COMMON MISTAKE: Sending back part of the difference as a compromise. Any amount sent by an irreversible method is lost the same way.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
A text says PayPal charged you $899 and gives a support number. You call and the agent asks you to install a remote access app to refund you.
Decision point 1: The invoice number matches the text. Do you proceed. Correct path: No. Hang up. A matching number only proves the same sender wrote both. Check PayPal in your own app instead.
Decision point 2: A refund appears that is $5,000 too large and the agent begs you to Zelle back the difference. Correct path: Send nothing. The display can be manipulated during access, and Zelle to a scammer is effectively irreversible.
Decision point 3: You already shared your screen for ten minutes. Correct path: Disconnect, call your bank on the number on your card, change passwords from a clean device, and preserve the messages for reporting.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Fake charge notices make you call the scammer
- The overpayment is faked with your own money or a reversible payment
- Never send back a difference by Zelle, wire, gift card, or crypto
- Never share your screen or install remote access for a refund
- Verify charges only in accounts you open yourself

GLOSSARY
Overpayment scam — A fake refund made too large so the victim sends back real money
Funds availability — A bank showing money as usable before the payment is fully collected
Remote access — Software that lets another person view and control your device
Charge confirmation — A notice of a purchase, which scammers forge to create alarm
Irreversible payment — A payment by wire, gift card, or crypto that cannot be recalled
Inbound call — A call you make to a number supplied by an unsolicited message
