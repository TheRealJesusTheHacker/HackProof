Structured Cabling & Physical Installs
MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Intermediate | Time: 60 minutes | Prerequisites: Basic networking concepts and safe hand-tool use.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Explain Cat6, Cat6a, and fiber, and when each fits
- Apply TIA standards awareness on a real job
- Plan pathways and terminations before pulling cable
- Test, label, and document an install others can trust
- Sequence an install day to sign-off

LESSON 1 — CABLE TYPES, STANDARDS, AND PATHWAYS
Structured cabling is the permanent wiring connecting jacks, cameras, and switches to a rack. Cat6 is the common choice for office drops, phones, cameras, and access points. Cat6a is thicker, harder to pull, and built for higher performance and longer high-speed runs. Fiber carries light, suits links between racks, buildings, and long distances, and needs different tools and care. Never bend fiber tightly, pull it like copper, or look into a live fiber end.

The Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA) publishes the standards manufacturers, testers, and BICSI training build on. Follow the current standard for bend radius, pull tension, separation, termination, and testing.

Plan the pathway first: ceiling space, conduit, tray, wall cavities, firestopping, and access. Never leave cable unsupported, kinked, or crushed.

CASE BREAKDOWN: An installer pulls Cat6 for twelve drops without a walkthrough. Two runs should have been a fiber link between racks, three land on the wrong wall for the furniture plan, and one pathway is blocked by ductwork. The crew re-pulls on its own time. A short survey and one question about furniture would have prevented it.

Pathway checklist:
- Confirm cable type, count, and destination for every run
- Walk and photograph the full pathway before pulling
- Label both ends before cable leaves the box or reel
- Respect bend radius and pull tension; support cable properly
- Terminate to the pinout on the plan, consistently at both ends

COMMON MISTAKE: Mixing pinouts between ends of a run. Use one scheme per the plan and test every run - a jack that looks right can still be wired wrong.

LESSON 2 — TERMINATIONS, TESTING, LABELING, INSTALL DAY
A termination is where cable meets a jack, panel, or connector. Strip only as much jacket as needed, keep pairs twisted close to the termination, seat conductors fully, and inspect your work. A sloppy punch-down fails only under load, making it expensive to find.

Test every run, not a sample. A continuity tester confirms wire order; a certification tester measures performance against the standard and produces client records. Fix and re-test before closing walls. Record results by cable label, not memory.

Label both ends, jack, and panel port with the same identifier, and put it on the as-built. Use durable printed labels.

Install-day workflow:
1. Check in with the client; confirm areas and hours
2. Walk the plan with your lead; flag changes first
3. Pull, support, and terminate in planned order
4. Test every run, correct failures, re-test
5. Label, photograph, update the as-built, clean up, get sign-off

CASE BREAKDOWN: A crew finishes a day early but labels nothing, planning to return. They never do. Months later a camera fails and a new technician spends half a day tracing one unlabeled cable.

COMMON MISTAKE: Testing only the hard runs. Easy runs get bad terminations too. Untested and unrecorded means not finished.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Plan on paper: a two-room office needs six drops, two access points, and a link between two racks.
Decision point 1: The rack link is long. Do you default to copper? Correct path: Measure first. Choose copper or fiber by distance, performance need, and the standard, and note the reason on the plan.
Decision point 2: The client wants a drop moved after the pull. Do you splice an extension? Correct path: No. Re-pull or re-route a proper run, terminate it correctly, and update plan and labels.
Decision point 3: Quick checks pass and the client wants to finish. Do you skip full testing and labeling? Correct path: No. Complete testing, labeling, photos, and the as-built first.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Choose Cat6, Cat6a, or fiber for the distance and job, not habit
- Follow current TIA requirements; confirm them when unsure
- Plan and walk every pathway before pulling cable
- Test, label, and document every run
- The as-built is part of what the client paid for

GLOSSARY
Structured cabling - Planned, permanent cabling inside a building
Drop - One cable run ending at a jack or device
Pathway - The route cable travels, such as conduit or tray
Termination - Where cable meets a jack, panel, or connector
As-built - Final drawing of what was actually installed
