MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~15 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Explain what quishing is and why a QR code hides its destination
- Inspect a QR code in public before scanning, especially on parking meters
- Preview a QR code destination before opening it on your phone
- Describe what to do if you entered credentials or payment details after a bad scan

LESSON 1 — THE CODE YOU CANNOT READ
A QR code is a shortcut, not a guarantee. Unlike a web address you can read letter by letter, a QR code hides where it leads until your phone opens it. Scammers exploit that blind spot. The attack is called quishing: QR code phishing. They place their own code over a real one, or post a convincing sign carrying only their code, and your phone takes you to a fake payment or login page. The most common public setup is the parking meter sticker.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A driver parks downtown, sees a QR sticker on the meter that says scan to pay, and scans it. The page asks for a card and phone number, which feels normal. She pays, sees a confirmation screen, and returns two hours later to a ticket for nonpayment. This could have stopped before she scanned, when a close look would have shown the sticker sat over printed text with rough, lifted edges. The signal most people miss is a QR code as the only payment option. Legitimate meters almost always offer more than one way to pay and name an app you can open yourself.

BEFORE YOU SCAN CHECKLIST: Look for a sticker over another sticker or printed sign. Feel the edges for a raised overlay. Check whether nearby signs name a specific app or website you recognize; if so, open that app directly. If anything looks tampered with, use a different meter or pay in person.

COMMON MISTAKE: Assuming a code in a public place must be official because it looks professionally printed. Printing a convincing sticker is cheap. Placement in public is not proof of legitimacy; it is often the whole trick.

LESSON 2 — FAKE PAGES, FAKE LOGINS, AND UNSOLICITED CODES
Quishing also arrives by email, text, and mail. A message may say a package could not be delivered, an account needs verification, a payment failed, or your workplace requires you to scan a code to update payroll details. The code leads to a polished fake page built to steal your password or card details. Your phone gives you one moment of defense: most phones preview the destination before fully opening it. Look for misspellings or a wrong domain. If it looks off in any way, close it.

CASE BREAKDOWN: An employee receives an email appearing to come from his payroll department, asking him to scan a QR code to confirm new direct deposit information before payday. He scans with his personal phone and lands on a page that looks like the company portal. He enters his username, password, and a verification code. This could have stopped at the preview, which showed a domain with the company name plus extra words, not the portal address he normally used. The signal most people miss is the channel switch. Real payroll changes are not confirmed by scanning a code in an unsolicited email on a personal phone.

WHAT TO DO AFTER A BAD SCAN: If you entered a password, change it from a known app or website, and anywhere you reused it. Turn on multi-factor authentication. If you entered card details, contact your bank and consider replacing the card. If a work credential was involved, tell your technology team at once.

COMMON MISTAKE: Scanning codes from unexpected messages because there is no visible link to inspect. Treat an unsolicited QR code exactly like an unsolicited link: do not use it.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
At a parking garage, the pay station has a handwritten sign taped above it: card reader broken, scan this code to pay, with a large QR code below. Decision point one: you are in a hurry and a line is forming. The correct path is to pause anyway. Hurry is what the setup depends on. Decision point two: you scan but submit nothing, and the preview shows a payment domain you do not recognize. The correct path is to close the page and pay another way. An unrecognized domain is a stop signal. Decision point three: another driver says he just used the code and it worked. The correct path is still to avoid it. A fake page can show anyone a confirmation screen while collecting card details in the background.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- You cannot read a QR code by looking at it. Its destination is hidden until you act.
- Check physical codes for stickers, overlays, and tampering before you scan.
- Read the destination preview and stop if the domain looks wrong.
- Treat unsolicited QR codes like unsolicited links: do not use them.
- Prefer the official app or a typed address over any code posted in public.

GLOSSARY
Quishing — Phishing delivered through a QR code that hides its destination
QR Code — A square barcode your phone reads as a link or instruction
Destination Preview — The address your phone shows before fully opening a scanned code
Overlay Sticker — A fake code placed over a real sign or code to redirect payments
Credential Harvesting — Collecting usernames and passwords through a fake login page
