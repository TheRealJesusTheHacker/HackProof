MODULE OVERVIEW
Level: Foundations | Time: ~20 minutes
Prerequisites: none

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
After this module you can:
- Recognize scams aimed at children in games, including free-currency offers
- Name secrecy from parents as a red flag and teach a child to act on it
- Set up code words and check-in habits that protect older relatives
- Build trust through conversation instead of relying on surveillance alone

LESSON 1 — PROTECTING CHILDREN WITHOUT TURNING HOME INTO A WATCHTOWER
Children meet scammers where they play. In games and chats, the common lure is free currency: coins, skins, or items in exchange for a login or a quick visit to an outside site. The site looks like the game, the child signs in, and the account is gone. Others offer a rare trade, then move the child to a private chat away from the game's reporting tools.

Teach two rules. First, free currency never comes from a stranger or a site outside the game. If an offer needs your password, it is not an offer, it is the theft. Second, secrecy is the alarm. Any message telling a child not to tell a parent means stop and tell an adult immediately.

Conversation protects better than surveillance alone. Monitoring can miss a chat inside a game, and a child who expects punishment will hide a mistake until it grows. Make the deal explicit: come to me early and we fix it together; you are not in trouble for being targeted. Agree purchases happen only in the official store, with your approval.

CASE BREAKDOWN: A friendly player offers a child free currency through a link. The page asks for the game login. The child enters it, gets an error, and says nothing, afraid of trouble. By the weekend the account is emptied. A phishing page harvested the login. It could have stopped at the link and the password request. The signal most people miss: the child's silence afterward was the scammer's real protection.

COMMON MISTAKE: Installing monitoring on a child's device without telling them. When they discover it, trust, your best early-warning system, is what gets damaged.

LESSON 2 — PROTECTING PARENTS AND OLDER RELATIVES
Older relatives are targeted with authority and fear: callers claiming to be from a bank, an agency, or a grandchild in trouble. The protection that works is not asking an elder to become a technology expert. It is a small system they can use while frightened.

Set up a family code word for any emergency request involving money, and agree a standing rule: no payment or account change happens from a single call. Your relative can always say, "My family handles money with me. I am going to hang up and call them." Put family numbers and the bank number from the back of the card on paper near the phone.

Rehearse it once, kindly, like a fire drill. Then check in regularly, because a relative may not mention a strange call unless asked. Praise the catches. If a relative does pay, fix first and blame never.

Say this when setting it up: "Scammers use professional scripts and fake voices now. The code word protects all of us, including me."

CASE BREAKDOWN: A grandmother gets a call from a crying young voice, then a man demanding bail money and secrecy. She remembers the family rule, hangs up, and calls her grandson's saved number. He is at work, safe. The rehearsed rule carried her through the moment when judgment was hardest. The signal most people miss: she did not need to decide whether the voice was real. The system decided for her.

COMMON MISTAKE: Giving an elder a long list of scam types to memorize. Under pressure, nobody recalls a list. One code word and one rule are enough.

HANDS-ON EXERCISE
Situation: Your ten-year-old shows you a message offering a rare item if she visits a claim site and signs in quickly, and asks you not to be mad.

Decision point 1: She clicked the link but did not sign in. Correct path: thank her for telling you, close the page together, and name what went right. Early honesty is the behavior you are building.
Decision point 2: She says the player told her it was their secret. Correct path: treat that as the lesson. Secrecy from parents is the red flag, and she was right to break it.
Decision point 3: Going forward, agree the two rules together, confirm purchases happen only with your approval in the official store, and change the account password together as practice, not punishment.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Free currency from a stranger or an outside site is a phishing lure.
- Secrecy from parents is a red flag a child should act on immediately.
- Conversation and early honesty protect children better than surveillance alone.
- Give elders one code word and one standing rule, with written contacts by the phone.
- Fix first and blame never. Shame creates secrecy.

GLOSSARY
Free-currency phishing — A lure offering game money or items to steal a login.
Game currency — Money-like units bought or earned inside a game.
Code word — A private word agreed in advance to confirm a family member's identity.
Standing rule — A decision made in advance that applies without debate in the moment.
Phishing page — A fake sign-in page built to capture entered details.
